## What it is

For most of human history, trust ran on cues: a familiar voice, a face, a
handshake, a storefront that was there last year and will be there next year.
Those cues worked because they were expensive to fake. Renting a building and
staffing it is a lot of effort for a con.

Online, trust means the same thing it always did — confidence that whoever's
on the other end is who they claim to be. What changed is the price of faking
the cues. An email that looks exactly like your bank's costs nothing. Caller
ID can be set to say anything. A voice that sounds like your relative can be
cloned from a short sample of audio. The instincts you inherited still fire —
*sounds right, looks official, feels urgent* — but the things they check are
now the cheapest part of the lie.

## Why it's a problem

Scams aren't really a technology problem; they're a trust problem. Every
scam, from the fake delivery text to the "grandma, I'm in trouble" call,
works the same way: borrow the *appearance* of someone you'd trust, then rush
you into acting before you check. The borrowed appearance does the opening,
and the urgency does the closing — because urgency is precisely the thing
that keeps you from verifying.

Notice what doesn't help here: being careful about your own information.
This is the situation where privacy and anonymity sit on the bench — you're
not the one who needs to be hidden. The other party is the one who needs to
be *proven*, and the modern internet gives you almost no built-in way to do
it. So for now, the proving is a habit you run yourself.

## What you can do

**Verify out-of-band.** If "your bank" calls or texts, hang up and call the
number on the back of your card. If "your kid" needs money urgently, call
them back on the number you already have. Real institutions and real
relatives survive this check every time; scams almost never do.

**Treat urgency as the tell.** "Right now, before it's too late" is the one
ingredient nearly every scam needs. Legitimate problems allow you the five
minutes verification takes.

**Never share codes.** A two-factor code is for the moment *you* log in.
Anyone who contacts you and asks you to read one out is performing the
takeover as you speak — no real company does this.

**Agree on a family code word** for money-or-emergency calls. It's the
cheapest voice-cloning defense there is, and it turns "sounds like family"
back into actual proof.

None of this requires expertise — just moving trust from the feeling to the
check. The feeling is what scammers rent. The check is yours.
