## What you'll be able to do afterward

- You'll know exactly what a network operator can and can't see about your browsing.
- Your phone stops volunteering to join every open network it passes.
- Your home network stops being the weakest link in the house.
- "Do I need a VPN?" gets an honest answer instead of an ad's answer.

## The plays

<ol class="plays">
<li>
<strong>Turn off auto-join for open Wi-Fi networks</strong>
<span class="path">Where: your phone's Wi-Fi settings — disable auto-join/auto-connect for open (no-password) networks</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: stopped your phone from silently attaching to any network named "Free_Airport_WiFi," operator unknown. You still can join open networks — it's now your decision, not the phone's.</p>
</li>
<li>
<strong>Use your own data for sensitive tasks on networks you don't control</strong>
<span class="path">When: banking, medical, work login — on hotel, café, or airport Wi-Fi</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: moved your most revealing traffic onto the network you pay for instead of the one somebody else runs. Cellular data isn't perfect, but its operator is at least a known quantity with a contract.</p>
</li>
<li>
<strong>Check for the padlock before typing anything sensitive</strong>
<span class="path">Where: the address bar — https and the lock icon</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: confirmed the network can see <em>which site</em> you're visiting but not <em>what you're doing there</em>. That's the real division of visibility: destinations are visible to the network, contents are not — as long as the padlock is present.</p>
</li>
<li>
<strong>Turn on private DNS if your phone offers it</strong>
<span class="path">Where: network or privacy settings — look for "Private DNS" or "encrypted DNS"</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: encrypted the name-lookups your phone makes, which are otherwise readable by whoever runs the network — a tidy list of every site you visit even when the traffic itself is locked.</p>
</li>
<li>
<strong>Set your home router's own password and encryption</strong>
<span class="path">Where: the router's admin page — change the default admin password, set Wi-Fi security to WPA3 (or WPA2)</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: closed the two default holes in the network everything you own connects to. A router still running its factory password belongs to whoever looks it up.</p>
</li>
<li>
<strong>Decide your VPN policy — deliberately</strong>
<span class="path">When: you regularly use networks you don't trust, or want your ISP out of your browsing</span>
<p class="did">What this just did: framed the real trade. A VPN doesn't make you anonymous — it moves your traffic's visibility from the network to the VPN company, so choose one you'd trust with that view, and treat "free VPN" as what it usually is: you're the product. On trusted networks with the padlock, you may not need one at all.</p>
</li>
</ol>

## You're now covered against…

- **Rogue and nosy networks** — nothing sensitive rides a network you didn't choose, and what does ride is padlocked with encrypted lookups.
- **Default-settings neighbors** — your home network no longer answers to a factory password.
- **VPN marketing** — you know what the tool actually does, so the ads have nothing to sell you.

Fifteen minutes, mostly decisions you make once. The network still sees a
little — it has to route you — but from here on, "a little" is all it gets.
